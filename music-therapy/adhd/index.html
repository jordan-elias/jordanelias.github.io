Music Therapy for ADHD

I offer individual music therapy for adults and teenagers with ADHD in Berlin and online. This work is grounded in a neurodiversity-affirming perspective — the starting point is not that something needs to be fixed, but that you deserve support that actually fits how your brain works.

Sessions are flexible, low-pressure, and shaped around your attention, energy, communication style, and goals. No musical experience is required. Learn more about music therapy and how it works, or read about online sessions if you are outside Berlin.

A Neurodiversity-Affirming Approach

Neurodiversity-affirming therapy begins from the position that ADHD is a natural variation in human neurology. The goal is not to help you function more like a neurotypical person, suppress the parts of yourself that don't fit conventional expectations, or feel permanently broken for struggling in environments that were not built with you in mind.

The goal is to understand yourself more fully, build genuine self-acceptance, and find ways of living and working that fit how your brain actually operates. That might mean identifying what conditions allow you to thrive rather than just cope, developing a less punishing relationship with yourself, or simply having a space where your attention is allowed to move the way it naturally does.

Music therapy is particularly well suited to this kind of work. Music engages attention in a direct, immediate way that doesn't depend on verbal discipline or the ability to sit with abstract concepts. For many people with ADHD, it offers a channel into emotional and reflective work that feels more natural and less effortful than traditional talk therapy.

Emotional Regulation and Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

Emotional dysregulation is one of the most significant — and least discussed — features of ADHD. It shows up in many ways: rapid mood shifts, difficulty calming down once activated, a low threshold for frustration, and the particular intensity of rejection sensitive dysphoria.

Rejection sensitive dysphoria, or RSD, is the experience of acute emotional pain in response to perceived criticism, failure, or rejection. It can be overwhelmingly intense, difficult to explain to others, and often mistaken for something else entirely. Many people with ADHD describe it as one of the most disruptive aspects of their daily life, shaping decisions, relationships, and self-perception in ways that are hard to disentangle.

Music therapy can offer a space to explore these responses without judgment. We might use music to map different emotional states, to identify what activation and deregulation actually feel like in the body, or to build a more flexible repertoire for moving between states. Over time, this can support a less reactive relationship with intense feelings — not by suppressing them, but by developing a richer understanding of what is happening and why.

Late Diagnosis and Reframing Your History

A late ADHD diagnosis often brings a complicated mix of feelings. Relief at finally having language for experiences that never quite made sense. Grief for the years spent struggling without support, being told to try harder, or blaming yourself for things that were never simply a matter of effort. And the significant work of looking back at your life and understanding it differently.

Many people describe seeing their history through a completely new lens after a diagnosis: the jobs that didn't work out, the relationships that were exhausting, the things they excelled at and the things they couldn't make themselves do no matter how much they wanted to. It is a lot to hold at once.

Music therapy can offer a space to work through that process without rushing it. Songs tied to particular periods of life can serve as anchors for memory and feeling. Creative work can give form to experiences that are hard to put into words. The work can move between reflection and practical support, at whatever pace feels right.

Attention, Focus, and the ADHD Brain

ADHD is not a straightforward deficit of attention. It is a different relationship with attention — one characterised by difficulty regulating it rather than simply lacking it. People with ADHD can hyperfocus intensely on things that genuinely engage them, and struggle enormously with tasks that don't, regardless of how important those tasks are.

Music has a direct and well-documented effect on attention. The right music at the right time can support focus, help with task initiation, make transitions less aversive, and create a more workable internal environment for sustained effort. But this is highly individual — what works powerfully for one person may be completely wrong for another.

Part of the work here is developing a more conscious and personalised understanding of your own relationship with sound and attention. What kinds of music support focus for you specifically? What is happening when you use music to regulate your state, and how can you use that more intentionally? This is practical work, and it is grounded in how your brain actually responds rather than in general advice.

Executive Functioning and Daily Life

Executive functioning difficulties are central to ADHD and affect almost every domain of daily life. Task initiation, time perception, working memory, planning, follow-through — these are areas where ADHD creates genuine and persistent challenge, in ways that are rarely proportionate to ability or intelligence.

Therapy can offer space to examine those challenges honestly and develop approaches that actually fit how your brain works, rather than strategies borrowed from neurotypical productivity frameworks that assume a fundamentally different kind of attention. Music-based tools — from structured listening practices to rhythm-based approaches to sequencing — can support specific areas of executive functioning in concrete ways.

This work can also involve processing the emotional weight of executive functioning difficulties: the shame, the frustration, the exhaustion of having to work so much harder than it looks like things should require. Understanding why something is hard is often an important part of being able to approach it differently.

Identity, Self-Worth, and the Long Shadow of Masking

Many people with ADHD spend years developing elaborate strategies to appear more organised, more consistent, and more in control than they actually feel. This takes many forms: over- preparing to compensate for anticipated failure, hiding the chaos beneath a polished surface, performing competence in ways that are exhausting to maintain. It is its own kind of masking, and like autistic masking, it comes at a significant cost.

The cumulative effect of years of this — alongside a history of criticism, underperformance relative to perceived potential, and internalised messages about being lazy, flaky, or unreliable — can leave deep marks on self-worth. Many adults with ADHD carry a chronic background sense of inadequacy that persists even when external circumstances improve.

Therapy here is not about positive thinking or reframing. It is about genuinely examining where those beliefs came from, what they are actually based on, and what a more honest and accurate relationship with yourself might look like. Music can support this in multiple ways: as a way of accessing emotional material that is hard to approach directly, as a domain where many people with ADHD experience flow and competence, and as something that carries identity and personal history in ways that words sometimes cannot.

Relationships and Social Experience

ADHD affects relationships in ways that are often poorly understood — both by the people around someone with ADHD, and sometimes by the person themselves. Difficulty with sustained attention in conversation, time blindness that reads as unreliability, emotional intensity that can be overwhelming, and the particular pain of RSD in the context of close relationships all create real challenges that conventional advice doesn't always address well.

Therapy here is not about learning to perform neurotypical social norms more convincingly. It is about understanding your own patterns honestly, developing clarity about what you actually need from relationships, and finding ways of communicating that feel authentic rather than constantly corrective.

For some people this might involve processing specific relational experiences — a pattern that keeps repeating, a friendship that didn't survive, a dynamic that was damaging. For others it is more about building a clearer sense of what sustainable relationships look like for them. Music can support both: as a subject of shared meaning, as a way of processing what has been difficult, or as a starting point for exploring how you connect and communicate.

Working with Music in Sessions

Sessions draw on several approaches depending on what is most useful for you at a given time. These might include:

  • Listening and playlist work: Building intentional playlists for focus, regulation, transitions, or emotional processing. Developing a conscious and personalised understanding of how different music affects your attention and internal state.
  • Verbal processing with music as anchor: Using songs, albums, or musical experiences as starting points for reflection and conversation. Particularly useful for accessing emotional material that is hard to approach directly, and for people who engage more easily with ideas when there is something concrete to attach them to.
  • Songwriting and composition: Using creative work to give form to experiences, feelings, or parts of identity that resist verbal expression. Projects can be as structured or as open as you prefer, and can draw on any level of musical experience.
  • Improvisation: Exploring sound freely as a way of externalising internal states, practising sustained attention, or simply playing without outcome. This is always optional and introduced gradually, never on demand.
  • Creative projects between sessions: Prompts and exercises to continue the work outside of sessions — listening with specific intentions, sound experiments, or longer creative projects that connect to your therapeutic goals.

You will never be required to perform, share creative work, or engage with music in a way that feels uncomfortable. Everything is optional and paced by you.
