Music Therapy for Grief and Bereavement

I offer individual music therapy for people navigating grief and loss, in Berlin and online. This work does not follow a model of grief as a problem to be resolved or a process to move through efficiently. It starts from the position that grief is a natural and necessary response to loss — one that deserves time, space, and support that does not rush it.

Sessions are shaped around where you are, what you are carrying, and what feels most useful right now. No musical experience is required. Learn more about music therapy and how it works, or read about online sessions if you are outside Berlin.

Music and Grief

Music has always been part of how human beings grieve. It is present at funerals and memorials, in the songs we reach for in the hours after a loss, in the recordings that carry a person's voice or represent a relationship. Music does something to grief that language often cannot: it holds the feeling without demanding that it be explained.

In therapy, this quality becomes something to work with deliberately. Music can open up emotional material that is otherwise inaccessible, provide a container for feelings that feel too large to hold in conversation, and offer a way of staying present with grief rather than moving around it. It can also carry connection — to the person who has died, to memories, to parts of yourself that existed in relation to what has been lost.

This does not mean sessions are primarily about music. Sometimes they are mostly conversation, with music as a quiet reference point. Sometimes a piece of music becomes the centre of an entire session. The work follows what is needed.

What Grief Actually Looks Like

Grief does not follow a neat sequence of stages. It is not linear, it does not have a predictable endpoint, and it does not look the same from one person to the next — or even from one day to the next for the same person. Some days it is acute and overwhelming. Others it is dull and exhausting. Some days it appears to have receded, and then something — a song on the radio, a smell, a particular quality of afternoon light — brings it back fully.

One of the most isolating things about grief is the pressure, often unspoken, to be progressing. To be doing better by now. To have found some peace or acceptance. Therapy here is not about moving you toward a particular emotional destination. It is about making space for grief as it actually is, for as long as it needs to be there.

That includes the parts of grief that are harder to talk about: relief, anger at the person who died, guilt for feeling something other than sadness, the complicated reality of mourning someone with whom your relationship was difficult. All of it belongs.

Continuing Bonds and Staying Connected

An older model of grief suggested that the goal was to detach — to let go of the person who had died in order to reinvest emotionally in the living. More recent understanding has moved significantly away from this. Many people find that maintaining a continuing bond with the person they have lost is not a sign of unhealthy grief, but a natural and sustaining part of how they carry their loss forward.

Music is one of the most powerful tools for this. It carries memory and relationship in a way that other forms of communication cannot replicate. Working with music that was shared with the person who died, or that represents something of who they were, can be a way of keeping them meaningfully present — not as a substitute for grief, but as part of it.

This might involve listening to music that was significant to them or to the relationship. It might involve creating something — a playlist, a piece of writing, a song — that holds them in some way. Or it might simply mean having a space where talking about them, playing their music, and remembering feels welcome rather than like something that needs to wind down.

Disenfranchised Grief

Not all grief is recognised by the people around us. The death of an estranged parent, a miscarriage, the end of a relationship that others didn't know about or take seriously, the loss of a pet, grief following a suicide — these are losses that can be minimised, overlooked, or met with discomfort rather than support. The person grieving is left not only with the loss itself, but with the additional weight of feeling that their grief isn't legitimate, or that they are not permitted to mourn in the same way others are.

This is sometimes called disenfranchised grief, and it is extremely common. Therapy offers a space where all losses are taken seriously, without hierarchy or judgment about what deserves mourning. There is no threshold of relationship, duration, or social recognition that your loss needs to meet here.

The same applies to losses that are not deaths: the end of a relationship or a marriage, a miscarriage or infertility, the loss of a friendship, the grief that can come with a significant illness or change in life circumstances. These are real losses, and they deserve real support.

Anticipatory Grief

Grief does not always begin with a death. When someone you love is dying — from a terminal illness, from a slow decline, from something that is taking them piece by piece — grief often begins long before the end. This is anticipatory grief, and it can be as consuming and as complicated as bereavement itself.

There is the particular exhaustion of caring for someone who is dying, of watching them change, of holding both love and loss at the same time. There is the loneliness of grieving someone who is still alive, which can feel impossible to explain and difficult to seek support for. And there is sometimes guilt — for looking ahead, for beginning to grieve, for the moments of relief or resentment or fear that come alongside love.

Therapy can offer space for all of it. For the caregiving, the anticipation, the complicated feelings, and the grief that is already present. Music can be particularly useful here for processing experiences that are still unfolding, and for finding ways to be present with the person you are losing while you still can.

Complicated Grief

For some people, grief does not soften over time in the way that is expected. The acute pain persists, intrudes into daily functioning, and resists the gradual integration that most people experience. This is sometimes called complicated grief or prolonged grief, and it is not a sign of weakness or failure to mourn correctly. It is simply grief that has become stuck, often for reasons that are understandable when you look at them closely.

Complicated grief is more likely when the loss was sudden or traumatic, when the relationship was itself complicated, when there was no opportunity to say goodbye, or when the grief has been suppressed or unsupported for a long time. It is also more common when multiple losses have accumulated, or when grief has become entangled with trauma, depression, or other significant difficulties.

Therapy here is not about forcing movement. It is about gently examining what might be keeping grief stuck, creating conditions where integration becomes possible, and offering support that is paced by what you can bear rather than by an external timeline.

Grief, Identity, and Meaning

Significant loss does not leave the self intact. Who we are is always partly constituted by our relationships — to people, to places, to roles and futures we had imagined. When those are lost, there is often a corresponding loss of self that goes alongside the grief. Who am I without this person? What does my life mean now? What do I do with the future I had been building toward?

These are not questions with ready answers, and therapy does not offer them. What it can offer is a space to sit with those questions without being rushed toward resolution, to explore what meaning might look like in the aftermath of loss, and to begin — slowly, without pressure — to find what remains and what might be rebuilt.

Music is particularly well suited to this kind of work. It can carry the past and the present simultaneously, hold contradiction and ambiguity without resolving it, and offer a language for things that are not yet ready to be said.

Working with Music in Sessions

Sessions draw on several approaches depending on what is most useful for you at a given time. These might include:

  • Listening and receptive work: Using music as a way of accessing and being present with grief — the music of the person who died, music that carries the relationship, or music chosen to hold a particular emotional state. Listening together can open up material that is otherwise hard to reach.
  • Verbal processing with music as anchor: Using songs, recordings, or musical memories as starting points for reflection and conversation. Music tied to specific people or periods of life can serve as a way into memory, feeling, and meaning that direct conversation sometimes cannot access.
  • Songwriting and composition: Creating something — a song, a piece of writing set to music, a playlist — as a way of memorialising, expressing, or processing what has been lost. This can be a powerful form of legacy work, and does not require any prior musical experience.
  • Improvisation: Exploring sound freely as a way of externalising grief, rage, tenderness, or whatever is present without the requirement of language or narrative. This is always optional and introduced gradually, never on demand.
  • Legacy and memorial projects: Longer creative work between sessions — building a musical tribute, curating recordings that represent the person who died, or creating something that can be shared with others who are also mourning.

You will never be required to perform, share creative work, or engage with music in a way that feels uncomfortable. Everything is optional and paced by you.

Who This Work Is For

This may be a good fit if you are:

  • Navigating the loss of someone significant — a person, a relationship, a life you had imagined
  • Grieving a loss that others have not fully recognised or supported
  • Caring for someone who is dying and already carrying anticipatory grief
  • Finding that grief has persisted or intensified rather than easing over time
  • Processing a loss that brought up complicated feelings — ambivalence, relief, anger, guilt
  • Looking for a space where you do not have to protect others from the weight of your grief
  • Someone who finds it hard to access grief through words alone
  • A teenager who has experienced loss and needs support adapted to their experience
  • Someone whose grief has become entangled with trauma, depression, or other difficulties

In Berlin and Online

Sessions are available in person in Berlin and online for English-speaking clients across Europe and internationally, including the United States. Online sessions can be particularly valuable for people who are in the early stages of loss and for whom leaving home feels like too much — the ability to be in a familiar, safe environment while doing this work matters.
