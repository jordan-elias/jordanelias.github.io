About me

I picked up a guitar for the first time when I was twelve. I don't remember deciding to take it seriously, it just became the place I went when things were hard. Somewhere in those early years, I learned something that has shaped everything since: that music could take something confusing or painful and give it a shape. Not fix it, exactly, but give it meaning.

I spent my twenties in Boston playing in bands, chasing sound more than any real plan. At some point the writing became more interesting to me than the performing, and I started finding composers who worked completely differently from how I'd been taught to think about music. John Cage handing decisions over to chance operations, to the I Ching, on purpose, refusing to control outcomes he easily could have controlled. I got pulled into ambient and electronic music. The more I sat with all of it, the more it seemed like these composers weren't only making interesting sound, they were working out something about control, about what happens when you stop forcing an outcome and let a process unfold on its own terms instead.

At that time I started reading Oliver Sacks and Daniel Levitin, and something clicked. Sacks writing about patients whose relationship to music survived injuries and conditions that took nearly everything else, Levitin unpacking what's actually happening in the brain when a piece of music moves you. Reading them was the first time I saw that this wasn't just personal, it was something you could actually study, and more than that, something you could do with other people. Music therapy, once I found the term for it, felt less like a discovery and more like something I'd already been circling for years without knowing what to call it. A way to bring psychology and music together that wasn't about performance or getting it right, it was about the process, about people connecting.

I moved to Berlin to go deeper into the neuroscience side of it, how music and sound actually work on the brain, how the environment we're in shapes perception and development, why a piece of sound can shift a nervous system state in ways that just talking about a feeling often can't manage. Underneath that, though, was something more personal.

I care about creativity being taken seriously as something more than a hobby or a personality trait. And I care, just as much, about minds that don't fit the world's expectations quietly. Music reaches both of these without needing to be translated into language first, which is a lot of why I ended up doing this work the way I do.

These days I work with musicians, writers, and other creative professionals building a life around their own output, with autistic and ADHD adults, and with people carrying anxiety, grief, or burnout. I'm based in Berlin. I see clients here in person, and online, wherever they happen to be.

Therapeutic Approach

My approach is collaborative, creative, and trauma-informed. Alongside talk-based processing, sessions draw on music-making, lyric analysis, songwriting, and improvisation as primary tools for emotional exploration and expression. Rather than applying a single fixed model, I work relationally, following each client's pace and interests.

A core focus of the work is understanding how past experiences shape present patterns of feeling and behaviour, and using creative methods to build new emotional language, self-awareness, and resilience. It starts with formulating goals together. The focus is process oriented: using music as a way to support those goals rather than aiming toward a polished product.

With musicians and creative professionals specifically, this often goes further into experimental and generative territory, chance operations, indeterminacy, Deep Listening, constraint-based exercises, drawing directly on my own background as a composer and musician working across Boston and Berlin.

My practice is explicitly neurodiversity-affirming and queer-affirming. Sessions are adapted to each individual's sensory, cognitive, and communication needs.

Education & Credentials

I hold a master's degree in cognitive neuroscience from Freie Universität Berlin and a bachelor's degree in psychology and music therapy from Berklee College of Music. I am board-certified by the Certification Board for Music Therapists (CBMT) — the independent body that sets the professional standard for music therapists in the United States and internationally. Earning the MT-BC credential required a supervised clinical internship of over 1,000 hours and passing the national board certification exam, and it requires ongoing continuing education to maintain. My graduate research investigated the impact of sound on stress using behavioural and neuroimaging methods, grounding my clinical work in a direct understanding of how music affects the brain and nervous system.
