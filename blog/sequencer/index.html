How to use the sequencer

The sequencer is a 16-step loop machine — the most common tool in electronic music production. Each of the 16 squares represents one sixteenth note: a beat subdivided into four equal parts, so four squares make up one beat and sixteen squares make one complete bar of 4/4 time. When you press play, a highlight moves through the squares from left to right, triggering any step that is switched on as it passes.

Tracks

The left column lists eight tracks: four rhythmic (Kick, Snare, Hi-Hat, Cymbal) and four melodic (Bass, Lead 1, Lead 2, Lead 3). Only one track is active at a time — click a track name to select it, then tap squares to turn steps on or off. The grid always shows the pattern for the selected track, so you build each layer separately. When you play, all eight tracks run simultaneously.

A good starting point is the example beat, which loads a classic 4-on-the-floor kick pattern, snare on beats 2 and 4, and eighth-note hi-hats. Press play to hear it, then click each track in turn and look at its pattern to understand what is contributing to the sound.

Rhythm tracks

The four rhythm tracks — Kick, Snare, Hi-Hat, Cymbal — are on/off: a step is either active or silent. The most fundamental drum pattern in 4/4 time places a kick on steps 1, 5, 9, and 13 (the four beats), a snare on steps 5 and 13 (beats 2 and 4), and hi-hats on every other step. From this foundation you can add, remove, or shift any step to create syncopation — rhythmic interest that pushes against or between the beats.

Melody tracks

The melodic tracks work slightly differently. When you tap an empty step, it turns on and a small menu appears showing the available notes from your chosen scale. Tap a note name to assign it. If you tap a step that is already on, the menu reopens so you can change the note or turn the step off. The key and scale selectors in the transport bar determine which notes are available.

The five scale options cover the most useful sounds in Western music. Major is bright and resolved. Natural minor is darker and more melancholic. Minor pentatonic — five notes from the minor scale — is particularly forgiving: almost any combination of steps sounds musical, which makes it ideal for improvising melodic ideas quickly. Major pentatonic is the equivalent bright five-note scale. Harmonic minor adds a raised seventh that gives a tense, dramatic quality.

Loop slots and chaining

The sequencer has four loop slots. Each stores a completely independent set of patterns across all eight tracks. By default, Loop 1 plays on repeat. Click Loop 2, 3, or 4 to switch to a different slot and program a new pattern — the copy button duplicates the current loop to the next slot so you can vary an existing pattern rather than starting from scratch.

The chain section lets you combine loops into an arrangement. Use the selectors to choose a loop and how many times it should repeat, then click Add. Export WAV renders the entire chain — or the current loop if no chain is set — as a stereo audio file.

Tempo

The BPM slider controls playback speed. 120 BPM is a common tempo for house and pop music. 90 BPM gives a more relaxed hip-hop feel; 140 feels urgent and energetic. Changes take effect immediately.

Sequencers in music therapy

The step sequencer is an unusually useful tool in music therapy contexts precisely because it removes the need for real-time performance skill. A client doesn't need to play an instrument to create a groove — they need only to make decisions: which steps should sound, which should be silent. That decision-making process is itself therapeutically rich. It involves attention, preference, aesthetic judgment, and the experience of cause and effect in a low-stakes, immediately audible domain.

The grid format also makes rhythmic structure visually legible in a way that standard music notation doesn't. You can see a rhythm as a pattern of filled and empty squares — symmetry and asymmetry, density and space. For clients who respond well to visual information, including many people with ADHD or autism, this visual-rhythmic mapping can make rhythmic concepts accessible in a way that verbal instruction alone cannot.

The randomize function is worth noting as a therapeutic tool in its own right. Handing an element of compositional control to chance — pressing a button and hearing what emerges — is a small experiment in indeterminacy, the same principle explored by composers like John Cage. The question of what to do with the result (keep it, clear it, modify it) opens up conversation about preference, tolerance for the unexpected, and the relationship between intention and outcome.
