Neural Garden: a network that learns to listen to itself

June 30, 2026

Written by Jordan Elias, MT-BC

Above is a small neural network. You build its first few connections by hand, and from there it keeps teaching itself — strengthening some of its own wiring, letting other parts fade, occasionally growing connections you never made. It expresses what it's doing as an ambient soundscape, and as a small creature that develops alongside it. Neither one is decoration; both are direct readouts of what the network is doing underneath.

What a neural network actually is

Strip away the mystique and a neural network is a small number of simple ideas, repeated and connected. A node (sometimes called a neuron, after the biological original) is a tiny unit that takes some input, decides how strongly to respond to it, and passes a signal onward. A connection is a link between two nodes, and every connection has a weight — a number that says how much influence one node has over the next. A weight near zero means "I barely affect you." A strong weight means "when I light up, you light up too."

That's most of it. A network's "knowledge" isn't stored anywhere special — it is the pattern of weights between its nodes. Learning, in almost every kind of neural network, just means adjusting those weights over time based on some rule. Different kinds of networks use different rules. This one uses one of the oldest and most intuitive: it strengthens connections between nodes that tend to activate together, and lets unused ones fade.

Activation function — the rule a node uses to turn its incoming signal into an outgoing one. In this lab, "sharply" (ReLU) means a node stays silent until input crosses a threshold, then switches fully on. "Smoothly" (sigmoid) fades gradually between off and on. "Both ways" (tanh) can swing in two directions, like a node that can either excite or suppress what it connects to. These aren't cosmetic settings — they're the actual functions used in real neural networks, doing the same job here that they do in any other one: shaping how a node responds rather than passing its input straight through unchanged.

Hebbian learning: the rule this network runs on

In 1949, the psychologist Donald Hebb proposed a simple theory about how the brain might wire itself based on experience, often summarized as "neurons that fire together, wire together." When two connected neurons activate at the same time, the synapse between them strengthens slightly. When they don't, it weakens. Run that rule over and over, across millions of neurons, and you get a brain that's gradually shaped by its own activity.

That's the literal mechanism driving the network above. Every connection tracks how often the two nodes it links are active at the same moment. Frequent overlap strengthens the connection; rarity lets it decay, and a connection that decays far enough is removed outright — the network actually forgets it, not just visually but in its underlying structure. This is also, not coincidentally, a real feature of biological brain development: synaptic pruning, the process by which a developing brain trims away connections it isn't using, keeping the ones that matter and discarding the rest.

This is worth distinguishing from the kind of learning you may have heard most about in AI news. Large language models and most modern deep learning systems learn through backpropagation — comparing their output against a correct answer and adjusting every weight in the network to reduce that error, over enormous amounts of labeled data. That requires a target to optimize toward. There's no "correct" soundscape here, so this network doesn't use that method. Hebbian learning is older, simpler, needs no labeled examples, and produces a network that organizes itself around its own patterns of activity rather than around a goal handed to it from outside.

What "mood" is doing

The mood reading reflects how consonant the network's current sound is — roughly, how well the pitches sounding at once agree with each other. It isn't just a display. It quietly steers two things: how readily the network forms spontaneous new connections between nodes whose activations have been aligning, and how much it explores versus settles. Pleasant-sounding states are treated as worth holding onto; rough patches are allowed to happen, because unpredictability is part of what keeps this alive over time, but they're nudged to resolve quickly rather than linger. This keeps the network usable as background sound without forcing artificial prettiness on it.

The creature

The pixel figure beside the graph isn't simulating anything separate from the network — it's a direct readout of the same underlying state, rendered as a growing form rather than a graph. It begins as a single cell. As the network accumulates nodes, forms connections, learns from its own co-activity, and simply stays alive longer, the creature passes through visible stages: eyes and a mouth appear, a tail grows, limbs develop, then a horn, then spines, then wing buds, and eventually something unmistakably dragon-like by the time the network has been running and self-organizing for long enough.

Because nothing here is saved, the creature you see is the only one that will ever exist in quite that configuration. A network left running and learning for twenty minutes in a background tab will sound and look meaningfully different from one shaped by hand for the same amount of time — and once you close the tab, both are gone.

Using it as background sound

Once you've built a network you like — a handful of nodes whose connections have settled into something pleasant — collapse the walkthrough, turn the volume to a level that sits comfortably under whatever else you're doing, and leave it running while you work, read, or rest. The system is deliberately tuned to avoid sudden loudness or jarring dissonance: the consonance signal corrects toward pleasant intervals, the voice count is hard-capped to prevent buildup, and spontaneous new connections form gently rather than with sharp transients. It's closer to weather than to foreground music. Checking in occasionally to nudge a new connection or see how the creature has grown is entirely optional.

Why build this as a music therapist

Generative ambient sound and exploratory creative tools come up often in music therapy practice, usually for reasons that have nothing to do with the underlying technology: a sound environment that shifts gently and unpredictably over time can hold attention differently than something static or fully predictable, and the act of shaping a system through small, direct actions has a different quality than passive listening. Understanding roughly how a neural network works has also become a fairly ordinary kind of literacy worth having. This page was an attempt to let those two things — a genuine working explanation of how a simple learning system behaves, and something pleasant to have on in the background of an afternoon — sit inside the same small object.
