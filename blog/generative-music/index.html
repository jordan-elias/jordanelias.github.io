Designing systems: Lucier, Radigue, and generative music

May 1, 2026

Written by Jordan Elias, MT-BC

What happens when a composer steps back from moment-to-moment decision-making and designs a process instead? Two tools above let you explore this question directly — one inspired by Alvin Lucier's iterative room recordings, one by Éliane Radigue's slowly evolving drones. The article below traces the ideas behind both, and considers what generative music has to offer as both an artistic philosophy and a therapeutic framework.

What is a generative system in music?

Most music, in the Western tradition at least, involves a composer specifying what sounds should occur: which notes, in which order, at which volume, for how long. The performer's job is to realise those specifications as faithfully as possible. The composer's hand is in every moment of the work.

Generative music inverts this relationship. The composer — or designer — creates a set of rules, conditions, or processes, and then allows those conditions to unfold without further intervention. The music emerges from the architecture of the system, not from moment-to-moment decisions. Brian Eno, who coined the term, described the experience of setting up generative systems and then encountering the output as a listener rather than an author — surprised by what his own rules had produced.

This is not the same as improvisation, where a performer makes real-time decisions. It is not the same as chance music either, where a composer uses dice or random operations to determine content. Generative music is deterministic at the system level — the rules are fixed — but unpredictable at the output level, because the interactions between elements produce results that were not explicitly composed. The composer designs the conditions for music to happen. The music happens.

Two composers explored this territory with particular depth and rigour: Alvin Lucier, who made the physical environment itself a generative instrument, and Éliane Radigue, who composed through the infinitely slow evolution of electronic drone.

Alvin Lucier and the room as instrument

In 1969, Alvin Lucier sat in a room and recorded himself reading a short text describing what he was about to do. He then played that recording back into the room through a speaker, and re-recorded the sound in the room. He played that recording back, re-recorded it again, and repeated the process dozens of times. Each iteration, the room acted as a filter — reinforcing certain frequencies that corresponded to its physical dimensions and absorbing others. Gradually, his voice dissolved. The room's acoustic fingerprint — its resonant frequencies, determined by the length, width, and height of the space — became audible as pure sustained tones. The speech transformed into music. The piece is called I Am Sitting in a Room.

What makes the work so elegant is that the room is not a backdrop. It is a compositional instrument. Every enclosed space has resonant frequencies called room modes — standing waves that form when a sound wave reflects between parallel surfaces and arrives back in phase with itself, reinforcing. The lowest mode depends on the longest dimension of the room: in a room 5 metres long, the lowest axial mode is around 34 Hz. Higher modes occur at integer multiples. These modes are normally inaudible in daily life, but Lucier's iterative process amplifies them dramatically. Each pass through the room's acoustic response squares the filter's effect — frequencies the room prefers become more prominent, others disappear. After many iterations, only the room's voice remains.

The text Lucier reads during the recording begins: "I am sitting in a room different from the one you are in now." Words — units of meaning — transform over iterations into sustained resonances that carry no semantic content. This is not a flaw. It is the point. The piece does not argue with the meaning of the words. It simply runs them through a process until the meaning falls away.

Try it yourself — beyond the browser

The Lucier module above simulates this process using digital filters. But the original process is even more interesting to try with real rooms and real equipment, and requires nothing more than a phone and some patience.

Record yourself saying a sentence — a single short phrase works best. Play it back through your phone speaker in a room. Record that playback on another device: a second phone, a laptop microphone, anything. You now have the first iteration. Play that recording back in the same room and record it again on the first device. Repeat this four or five times. If you are consistent with placement — keeping the speaker and the recording microphone in roughly the same positions — you will hear the room's resonant frequencies beginning to emerge. The closer the recording microphone is to a wall or corner, the more the room modes will be reinforced, because those are the locations where standing waves have maximum amplitude.

A few variables to experiment with: different rooms (bathroom, stairwell, and large hallway are all interesting), different source materials (speech, singing, a single sustained note), and different distances between speaker and microphone. Each configuration produces a different outcome because it engages the room's acoustic properties differently. The process is consistent; the environment is the variable.

Éliane Radigue and the art of slow time

Éliane Radigue spent decades composing music that refuses to announce itself. Her works, largely created with an ARP 2500 synthesizer and later with acoustic instruments, unfold over extremely long durations — often exceeding an hour — with changes so gradual that individual moments of transformation are difficult to locate. The music does not have events in the conventional sense. It has states that imperceptibly become other states.

Radigue came to music through Pierre Schaeffer and Pierre Henry's Paris studio, working as an assistant before developing her own practice. She was deeply influenced by Tibetan Buddhism — she became a practitioner in 1975 — and the connection between her musical philosophy and Buddhist ideas about time, impermanence, and sustained attention is explicit in her own statements about the work. She described composing as a form of meditation practice, attending to the smallest fluctuations in the sound with the same quality of presence she brought to formal practice.

The technical basis of her drone compositions is deceptively simple: two or three oscillators tuned to nearly identical frequencies, whose slight differences produce slow beating — an amplitude modulation at the difference frequency. If two oscillators are tuned to 110 Hz and 112.5 Hz, they produce a beat at 2.5 Hz — a slow, barely perceptible pulse that waxes and wanes. As the oscillator frequencies drift slightly over time, the beat frequency changes, and with it the entire texture of the sound. The mathematics is elementary. The perceptual result, sustained over long duration with careful attention, is surprisingly complex. The analogue noise floor of her vintage equipment — which she considered part of the sound — added a thin layer of texture that gave the tones a sense of materiality and presence absent from a pure digital sine wave.

Generative systems as psychotherapy

There is a way of understanding both Lucier's and Radigue's approaches that maps directly onto central ideas in contemporary psychotherapy — not as a loose metaphor, but as a structural correspondence that illuminates something about how therapeutic change actually works.

Lucier and cognitive defusion. In Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, one of the core processes is called cognitive defusion — the practice of creating distance between a person and their thoughts so that thoughts can be observed as thoughts rather than fused with as literal truth. When someone is strongly fused with a thought — "I am fundamentally inadequate," "I will always be alone" — it functions not as a belief that could be questioned but as a transparent fact about reality. Defusion techniques create perceptual distance: the thought is repeated until its meaning fades, or observed as passing weather rather than permanent climate, or heard as words rather than experienced as statements about the world.

The Lucier process is a physical enactment of defusion. A sentence — a unit of semantic meaning — is run through a process that gradually strips away its meaning until only its resonant structure remains. The words dissolve. What was language becomes sound. The piece does not argue with the sentence's meaning, does not challenge or reframe it. It simply runs it through a system — of attention, of repetition, of gentle observation — until the meaning falls away and something underneath becomes audible. This is, structurally, what defusion asks the mind to do with painful thoughts.

Radigue and present-moment awareness. The central practice of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction is deliberately simple: direct your attention to what is happening now, with curiosity and without judgment. When attention wanders, notice that it has wandered and return. The skill being developed is not the absence of mind-wandering but the noticing of it, and the capacity to return without self-criticism.

Radigue's music demands exactly this quality of attention. Nothing announces itself. There are no events to mark, no melodies to follow, no rhythmic pattern to anchor the ear. What there is: a sustained field of sound that changes so slowly the changes are perceptible only if you are genuinely present for consecutive moments. If your attention wanders and returns, you will find yourself unable to locate the change that occurred in your absence. The music rewards the same thing mindfulness practice develops: non-grasping, present-moment attention without expectation of dramatic content.

There is also something important about the scale of duration. An hour-long drone does not permit the kind of engagement that expects resolution within a few minutes. It requires a different relationship with time — one where something can be present and valid without progressing toward a conclusion. This is therapeutically significant for people who struggle with the sense that their inner life is stuck, or that nothing is changing, or that growth must be perceptible to be real.

The generative frame and the therapeutic relationship. More broadly, the model of the composer as system designer rather than moment-to-moment decision-maker maps onto a particular understanding of what the therapist does — and what they do not do. The therapist does not produce therapeutic change directly. They design conditions: the quality of the relationship, the frame of the work, the specific practices or invitations offered. Within those conditions, change happens — or it does not. The therapist, like the generative composer, is responsible for the architecture. The music that emerges is not theirs to control.

Experiments

Lucier — listening to meaning dissolve

Use the generated voice source. Press play, then press iterate once and play the result. Track what changes with each step. At what point does it stop sounding like speech? At what point does it start sounding like music? Is there a moment when neither description fits?

Then try recording your own voice using the microphone option. Say a single sentence — something short and emotionally neutral, or something that carries some weight for you. Run it through eight or ten iterations. Notice what relationship you have to the sentence at iteration 0 compared to iteration 10. Does anything change about how it feels to hear those words processed into pure resonance?

Lucier — the loop and the mantra

Record a short phrase and enable loop mode. Let a single iteration play on a continuous loop for two or three minutes. This approximates what happens in certain contemplative practices where a phrase is repeated until its semantic content fades and what remains is something more purely sonic. Notice whether the meaning of the words changes under repetition, and whether that change is welcome or unsettling.

Radigue — attending to slow change

Start the drone and set a timer for five minutes. Close your eyes. Your only task is to track what is changing in the sound — not to name it or evaluate it, but to stay in contact with it. When attention wanders, return. After five minutes, notice whether your relationship to the sound changed over that duration, and whether your experience of time shifted. The drone is designed so that something will have changed from the beginning, but gradually enough that you cannot identify the exact moment it happened.

Radigue — the noise floor as presence

Bring the noise slider to maximum. Listen for several minutes. Then bring it to zero. What changes about the character of the sound? Many people find the tones feel thinner or more exposed without the noise floor beneath them. The noise carries no pitch, no structure, no pattern — but it creates a sense of materiality, of the sound existing in a physical medium. This distinction maps onto something in human experience as well: the difference between a presence that fills a space and an absence that merely occupies it.

A note on therapeutic applications

Neither of the tools above is a clinical intervention in itself. What they offer is experiential access to ideas that are therapeutically relevant: that meaning is not fixed in material, that attention to slow change develops a capacity for presence, that designing good conditions and then releasing control of the outcome is both an artistic practice and a relational one.

In sessions, I have used iterative audio processing of a client's own recorded voice as a way of creating literal distance from the content of negative self-statements — running the Lucier process on words that carry painful associations and listening as those words become frequencies become silence. This is not a technique I would use with everyone, or early in a therapeutic relationship. But for some people, at the right moment, the perceptual experience of meaning dissolving is more compelling than any cognitive reframe.

The Radigue drone as a backdrop for body-oriented work is more broadly applicable. The sustained tones, because they have no rhythmic structure and no melodic development, make no demands on cognitive attention. They create an acoustic field without pulling the person out of their body and into listening. Much background music has enough structure to engage narrative-processing systems — which is the opposite of what somatic awareness work requires. These tones are different.
