Knitting machines as musical instruments

June 18, 2026

Written by Jordan Elias, MT-BC

A knitting machine punch card is a binary grid. Each position is either punched or not. Each column is read in sequence as the carriage moves across the needle bed. A MIDI piano roll is also a binary grid. Each event is either on or off. Each column is a moment in time, read in sequence by a sequencer. The structural identity between these two systems is not coincidental — both are forms of notation that encode instructions for a process unfolding through time. But the connection goes deeper than that. Both systems share the same grammar of pattern: the operations of repetition, retrograde, inversion, and retrograde-inversion that composers have used for centuries are the same operations that generate the most complex and beautiful woven fabrics. This tool is built around that shared grammar.

The card before the computer

In 1804, Joseph Marie Jacquard demonstrated a loom that could weave any pattern, however complex, by reading a sequence of punched cards. The heddles — the mechanisms that raise and lower the warp threads — were controlled by a chain of cards whose holes determined which threads were lifted for each pick of the shuttle. A complex brocade that had previously required a skilled drawboy to manually select threads for every row could now be produced by any operator, because the knowledge was encoded in the cards.

Charles Babbage saw a Jacquard demonstration and recognised something in the cards: they were a form of programming. His Analytical Engine, designed in the 1830s but never built, used punched cards adapted from the Jacquard system to store both data and operations. Ada Lovelace, writing about the Engine, made the connection explicit: the Analytical Engine weaves algebraic patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves. The binary logic that underlies every computer is a direct descendant of the binary logic of warp-up and warp-down.

The domestic knitting machines that became popular in the 1960s and 70s — Brother, Silver Reed, Toyota — used a simplified version of this mechanism. A card 24 stitches wide, read by a row of pins as the carriage passes, selects needles for patterning. The 24-column constraint derives from the physical needle bed: most standard-gauge machines have beds that are multiples of 24, so a 24-stitch card tiles perfectly across any width. This constraint is generative in the same way a sonnet's 14 lines are generative — it doesn't restrict what can be expressed, it shapes the vocabulary in which it gets expressed.

Pattern as transformation

In music, the most rigorous treatment of pattern as transformation came with the twelve-tone technique developed by Schoenberg in the 1920s. A tone row — an ordering of all twelve chromatic pitches — could be subjected to four operations: prime (the original ordering), retrograde (reversed), inversion (each interval direction flipped), and retrograde-inversion (both). These four forms are related by a precise algebraic structure: they form what mathematicians call the Klein four-group, the smallest non-cyclic group, in which every element is its own inverse and any two elements combine to produce the third.

The same four operations apply directly to a punch card. The prime is the card as drawn. The retrograde is the card read right to left — in music, the phrase played backwards; in knitting, the pattern mirrored horizontally. The inversion is the card flipped upside down — in music, every interval inverted; in knitting, the pattern mirrored vertically. The retrograde-inversion is both flips applied. Bach used all four systematically in his fugues and canons. The tool above lets you hear all four in sequence and watch what each does to the grid in real time.

What makes this more than formal curiosity is that these transformations reveal relationships between patterns that are invisible to direct inspection. Two cards that look very different may be related by a retrograde or inversion, and when they are, they share a deep musical and structural kinship. Applying a transformation and listening to the result is a way of hearing the hidden symmetry of a pattern designed primarily for its visual properties.

Interval complementarity

When a card is inverted — flipped vertically — every pitch is replaced by its complement within the chosen scale. If row 3 sounds a minor third above the root, its mirror row sounds a minor third below, which within the octave is a major sixth above. The minor third and the major sixth are interval complements: they sum to twelve semitones. Every interval has a complement: a major second and a minor seventh, a perfect fourth and a perfect fifth, a tritone and itself. The tritone is its own complement, which is why it has a peculiar kind of symmetry — inversion leaves it unchanged.

This relationship arises from the arithmetic of the octave. The operation of inversion maps every pitch to its mirror image around the octave's midpoint. What inversion sounds like depends on the scale: in a major scale, the first degree maps to itself, the second to the seventh, the third to the sixth, the fourth to the fifth. In a pentatonic scale the mapping is different; in a whole tone scale, every pitch maps to another pitch in the same scale.

The complement toggle in the tool makes this visible in real time. When you punch a hole at row R, its complement is ghost-highlighted at the mirror row. Punching the complement as well creates a pattern that, when inverted, produces exactly the same holes in the same positions. Designing with this constraint produces patterns with a specific visual quality — they look the same upside-down — and a specific musical quality: their prime and their inversion are identical.

Self-inverting scales

Some scales have the property that their inversion is identical to themselves, or to a transposition of themselves. These are the scales that produce the most striking results when you flip the card and listen: the music doesn't change, or changes only in a way that reveals the symmetry rather than obscuring it.

The whole tone scale — six pitches each a whole step apart — is perfectly self-inverting. Every interval in the scale is a whole tone, so inverting any interval produces another whole tone. A rising major third inverts to a falling major third, which within the scale is equivalent to a rising minor sixth. The scale sounds identical read upside-down. This means a card designed using the whole tone scale produces music from its inversion that is in the same harmonic language as the prime, just rearranged. Debussy used the whole tone scale extensively, partly for this quality of hovering suspension: without the asymmetry of a leading tone, there is no gravitational pull toward resolution.

The diminished scale — alternating semitones and whole tones — has not one but two axes of inversional symmetry, at the tritone. Transposing it by a tritone produces the same scale. This makes it the foundation of a great deal of symmetrical harmony in jazz and twentieth-century classical music. It produces particularly rich results when tiled: the pattern that appears when a diminished-scale card is repeated has a visual density and regularity that directly reflects the scale's mathematical structure. The visual and the sonic complexity are expressions of the same underlying symmetry group.

Try the experiment: select whole tone or diminished in the scale panel, draw a motif, press play, then apply the I transformation and play again. With a self-inverting scale, the two should sound like variations of each other. With a major or minor scale, they will sound more distinctly different. That difference in perceived relatedness is the perceptual signature of the scale's symmetry properties.

Tiling as compositional grammar

A single motif cell is not a complete knitting pattern any more than a single measure of music is a complete piece. The pattern emerges from how the motif is repeated across the width of the card and down its length. The basic tiling operations — straight repeat, mirror repeat, half-drop — are also musical operations.

A straight repeat is ostinato: the same material returning without transformation. A mirror-X tile alternates the motif with its horizontal reflection, producing the kind of bilateral symmetry found in a palindrome — a phrase that reads the same forwards and backwards. Mirror-Y alternates the motif with its vertical reflection across rows, producing a pattern that in music corresponds to what theorists call an axis of inversional symmetry: the pitch content reflects around a fixed centre. Mirror-XY — four-way symmetry — produces both simultaneously, which in the piano roll corresponds to a passage that is simultaneously its own retrograde and its own inversion. This is the maximum constraint: the motif completely determines its own transformations. It also produces the most visually regular fabrics, the dense allover patterns characteristic of traditional Fair Isle and Jacquard textiles.

The brick-drop or half-drop repeat offsets alternate columns by half the motif height. This is a spatial canon: the same material appearing at a time offset, overlapping with itself. The canons in Bach's Musical Offering work on exactly this principle. The effect in both fabric and music is of a texture in motion, a pattern that seems to travel even though each element is fixed.

Working with the tool

The workflow moves between textile and musical thinking without privileging either. Start by choosing a motif size: 8×8 is a good default, large enough to contain interesting patterns, small enough that the tiling produces genuine repetition within the 24-column card. Draw in the motif cell on the left; the full card updates in real time on the right. Choose a tile mode and listen immediately — the same motif tiled in different ways produces very different music, and the difference is audible before you can articulate it.

The interval complement toggle makes the inversional structure visible as you draw. When it is on, every hole you punch ghost-highlights its complement. Designing a motif with complement awareness — choosing whether to punch the complement or leave it unpunched — is a way of making structural decisions about the music while thinking visually. Punching both a hole and its complement creates redundancy under inversion. Leaving the complement unpunched creates asymmetry. Mixing both strategies produces patterns where some intervals are symmetrical and others are not.

The transformations module lets you apply all four classical operations individually, or play them in sequence with the P → R → I → RI button. This sequence is worth doing with any pattern you find interesting: it reveals which transformations feel closely related to the original and which feel like a different piece. That sense of relatedness is not subjective — it tracks the degree to which the motif and its transformation share structural properties. Motifs with more internal symmetry produce transformations that are more recognisably related.

The export function produces a print-ready version of the card at 1:1 scale, with holes spaced 5 mm centre-to-centre to match the standard Brother and Silver Reed pin spacing. Print at 100% scale with no fit-to-page scaling, cut along the border, and the card is ready for the machine. If motif reference is enabled, the sheet includes a small diagram of the motif cell — useful for hand-drawing additional cards or for understanding where in the tiled pattern a given design decision originated.

Constraint and the generative value of limits

Both knitting machines and twelve-tone composition work within strict constraints: the 24-column width, the fixed set of twelve pitches, the four transformations. A common response to constraint is to experience it as restriction. But the more interesting relationship with constraint is to experience it as the thing that makes creativity possible at all.

Without the 24-column limit, a punch card would be a shapeless sequence. The limit forces the pattern to tile, and tiling is where the most interesting visual and musical relationships emerge. Without the scale constraint, all intervals are available and none carry particular weight. The constraint is what makes certain intervals feel surprising and others expected, what makes retrograde sound like a return and inversion sound like a reflection.

The Klein four-group — the algebraic structure underlying the four transformations — is not imposed on the material from outside. It is latent in the grid itself. Any binary matrix has a prime, a retrograde, an inversion, and a retrograde-inversion, and those four are always related in exactly this way regardless of what the matrix contains. The composer or knitter does not choose whether to have a symmetry group. They choose which element of the group to work with at any given moment, and how explicitly to acknowledge the others.

In a therapeutic context, this matters in a specific way. People experiencing perfectionism or creative anxiety often interpret the limits of a situation — the time available, the materials to hand, the skills they have — as obstacles to making something good. The knitting machine with its punch card offers a direct, embodied experience of the opposite: the limits are not obstacles, they are the grammar. The pattern is not produced in spite of the 24 columns. It is produced because of them. Working with that experience — noticing that constraint and creativity are not opposites — is something this kind of making can offer that conversation sometimes cannot.

Experiments

Sound before sight

Load the diamond preset and close your eyes before pressing play. Listen for a minute with loop on. Form an impression of the musical character: dense or sparse, rhythmically even or syncopated, melodic or chordal. Then open your eyes and look at the card. Does the visual distribution of holes correspond to your sonic impression? Now switch the tile mode without changing the motif and listen again. The same motif, differently tiled, will sound like a different composition.

The whole tone inversion

Select the whole tone scale. Draw an asymmetric motif — something with no obvious symmetry. Press play and listen. Then press I in the transformations panel and play the inversion. Compare. Then press P → R → I → RI and hear all four in sequence. With the whole tone scale, prime and inversion should sound like they inhabit the same harmonic world. Repeat with the major scale: the inversion will sound more distinctly other. That shift in how related the transformations sound is a direct perception of the scale's symmetry properties.

Draw with complements

Enable the complement toggle. Draw a motif using only holes whose complements you also punch — for every hole, punch its ghost. The resulting card will be its own inversion: flipping it vertically produces an identical card. Apply I in the transformations panel. The music should be the same. Then draw an asymmetric motif with complement off and compare. The difference between a card that is its own inversion and one that is not is audible once you know how to listen for it.

Melody into fabric

Think of a short melody you know well — four to eight notes. Map each note to a row: the lowest note goes to the bottom rows, the highest to the top. Map each beat to a column. Punch the motif accordingly and listen. You now have a knitting pattern that encodes a melody. Change the tile mode to mirror-X: you have made a palindrome of your melody. Apply R: you are hearing it backwards. Export the card and knit it. You will be wearing music you know.

Group score

Each participant in a workshop designs a motif independently. The facilitator sets one tile mode and one scale for the whole group. Cards are played back before participants see each other's motifs — the group hears the compositions without knowing their source. Then cards are printed and knitted. The resulting fabrics share a structural grammar but look and feel entirely different because the motif is different. Laid side by side, the comparison becomes a conversation about what is shared and what is individual in a common language.
