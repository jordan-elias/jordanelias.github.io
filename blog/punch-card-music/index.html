Knitting machines as musical instruments

June 18, 2026

Written by Jordan Elias, MT-BC

A knitting machine punch card is a binary grid. Each hole is either punched or not. Each column is read in sequence. The machine uses this information to decide which needles to select and in which order, producing a pattern in the fabric. A MIDI file is also a binary grid. Each event is either on or off. Each column is a moment in time. A sequencer uses this to decide which notes to play and in which order, producing a pattern in sound. The structural identity between these two systems is not a coincidence. Both are forms of notation. Both encode instructions for a process that unfolds through time. This tool lets you edit one and hear the other.

What is a punch card?

Domestic knitting machines — most famously those made by Brother, Silver Reed, and Toyota from the 1960s onwards — use a mechanism called a patterning carriage that reads a card as it passes through. The card is a strip of stiff paper or mylar with holes punched at specific positions across a fixed width, typically 24 stitches for standard-gauge machines. As the carriage moves across the needle bed, a row of pins reads the card: a hole allows the pin to drop, selecting the needle for pattern colour or stitch variation; a solid position holds the pin up, leaving the needle in its default position.

The 24-column constraint is not arbitrary. It derives from the physical width of the needle bed and the requirement that patterns repeat evenly across it. Because most knitting machine needle beds are multiples of 24, a 24-stitch card tiles perfectly across the full width. This constraint produces a characteristic visual grammar: patterns tend toward geometric forms, symmetrical repeats, and regular intervals. The limitation is generative in the same way a sonnet's 14-line constraint is generative — it does not restrict what can be said, it shapes the vocabulary in which it gets said.

Each row of the card advances when the carriage completes one pass across the fabric. Rows on a punch card correspond to rows of knitting, accumulating downward into the finished fabric. A pattern that takes 16 rows to complete will repeat throughout the length of the garment each time the card cycles.

The grid as score

Musical notation, like punch cards, is a system for encoding instructions that will be read sequentially by a performer or machine. Western staff notation encodes pitch on the vertical axis (position on the staff) and time on the horizontal axis (position in the measure). Piano roll notation, which became the visual language of MIDI sequencers and digital audio workstations, uses exactly the same convention: vertical position indicates pitch, horizontal position indicates when a note occurs.

A punch card read as a piano roll assigns pitch to each row and time to each column. A hole in row 1, column 3 means: on the third beat, play the pitch assigned to row 1. Rows near the top of the card produce higher pitches; rows near the bottom produce lower ones. The overall distribution of holes across the card — how dense, how sparse, how clustered — translates into the rhythmic and harmonic texture of the music.

This mapping produces results that are genuinely surprising. Patterns designed for their visual properties in fabric — diamonds, chevrons, geometric allover prints — often produce unexpectedly coherent musical phrases. Conversely, starting from musical ideas and working backwards to their punch card representation can generate knitting patterns unlike anything you would design directly. The translation is lossless in the sense that no information is discarded; it is creative in the sense that meaning is radically transformed.

How the tool works

The grid editor above shows a 24-column, 16-row punch card. Clicking a cell toggles it between punched (filled) and unpunched (empty). Dragging across the grid allows you to draw or erase multiple holes in a single gesture. The pattern presets generate starting configurations: diamonds and chevrons produce the symmetric repeats typical of traditional machine knitting; random generates a distribution that tends toward atonal clusters; stripes and checkers produce repetitive rhythmic patterns when played back.

The symmetry tools mirror whatever you draw in real time. Horizontal symmetry reflects across the vertical centre axis of the card, producing patterns that look the same from left to right — useful for allover patterns. Vertical symmetry reflects across the horizontal centre, producing patterns that reverse as they travel down the fabric. Both axes active produces four-way symmetry, which corresponds to the most classically geometric punch card patterns.

During playback, the card is read left to right like a player piano. Each column represents one beat at the chosen tempo. The 24 rows map to 24 pitches in the chosen scale, ascending from bottom to top. All holes in a column that is currently being read trigger simultaneously, so a column with many holes produces a chord, a column with one hole produces a single note, and a column with no holes produces silence. The playhead position is indicated on the grid in real time.

The export function produces a print-ready version of the card at 1:1 scale, with holes spaced 5 mm centre-to-centre to match the standard Brother and Silver Reed pin spacing. Print at 100% scale with no fit-to-page scaling, cut along the border, and the card is ready to use with a compatible machine.

Pattern, time, and attention

There is something worth attending to in what happens when you listen to a punch card played back. The 24-column repeat means the musical phrase is short — at 90 BPM it lasts about 16 seconds before cycling. But within that cycle, small changes in the distribution of holes produce large changes in the texture of the music. A single hole moved one row changes the chord voiced on that beat. A single hole moved one column changes the rhythm of a melodic line.

This sensitivity to small changes is a property of complex systems generally, and it is one reason both musical and textile notation are interesting to study. The instructions are simple and discrete; the outputs are complex and continuous. A punch card that looks sparse on paper can produce surprisingly dense harmonic material when played. One that looks visually complex can produce something rhythmically very sparse if the holes cluster on one axis.

The knitting group context adds another dimension. When several people are working from the same card — or from cards that encode the same musical phrase — they produce different fabric depending on yarn, tension, and gauge, just as different musicians playing from the same score produce different performances. The score is shared; the interpretation is individual. This balance between constraint and freedom is something both improvising musicians and hand knitters navigate constantly.

Practical notes for the workshop

For a workshop context, the most generative starting point is usually a pattern designed for its visual properties, played back before participants have seen the grid. The question of what something sounds like before you see where the holes are, and then whether you would have guessed from looking at the grid, tends to produce good conversation about the relationship between visual and sonic pattern, and about what we mean when we say a pattern is complex or simple.

A few patterns that work particularly well as both fabric and music: diamonds (produces a clear melodic arch that rises and falls symmetrically), checkers (produces an alternating two-note rhythm that recalls minimalist tape music), and the wave preset (produces a stepwise melodic line that moves slowly up and down the scale). The random preset is worth using to demonstrate how atonal distributions produce music that is neither rhythmically nor melodically predictable, even when the fabric it would produce has a perfectly even density.

For participants who want to design from the musical side: starting from a simple folk melody or a memorable phrase from a song, transcribing its rhythm and approximate pitch contour into holes on the grid, and then knitting the resulting card, produces a garment that is literally a song made into fabric. The challenge is mapping the melody's pitch range onto the 24 available rows, which requires some compromise — a useful constraint that focuses attention on which aspects of the melodic line are essential.

Experiments

Sound before sight

Load a preset without looking at the grid. Play the pattern on loop for a minute with eyes closed. Form an impression of its musical character — dense or sparse, rhythmically even or syncopated, high or low in register. Then open your eyes and look at where the holes are. Does the visual distribution of the holes match your impression? Can you see the musical character in the card now?

One change at a time

Start with the diamonds preset and enable loop. While it plays, click a single hole to add or remove it. Listen to what changes. Then click it again to restore the original. Try this with holes in different positions — a hole in the centre of the card, a hole on the edge, a hole that completes a chord versus one that creates a dissonance. The aim is to develop a feel for how grid position translates into sonic consequence.

Write a melody into fabric

Think of a short melody — four to eight notes — that you know well. It could be a folk tune, a childhood song, a phrase you find yourself humming. Map each note to a row: the lowest note in the melody goes near the bottom of the grid, the highest near the top. Map each beat to a column. Punch the grid accordingly. Play it back. You now have a knitting pattern that encodes a melody. Export the card and consider what it would mean to wear music you know.

Group score

In a workshop setting: each participant designs a four-column section of the card and keeps it secret. The facilitator assembles them in sequence to produce a 24-column card. Play the result before anyone has seen the complete grid. The music is a composition made by the group without any single person knowing the whole. Then reveal the assembled card. Discussion: which section did you recognise as yours? Did anyone's section change meaning when you heard it in the context of others?
